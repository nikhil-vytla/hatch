# Self-efficacy: when a goal feels out of reach

Read this when the user, or you, wants to lower a rung because it "seems impossible". The source article's closing argument is that people have always been held back by what they believe is possible. Each new model generation widens what's possible faster than those beliefs catch up.

## Evidence worth citing to the user

- **Specific, hard goals beat "do your best."** Locke and Latham's review of about 35 years of goal-setting research found that specific, difficult goals reliably lead to better performance than vague encouragement. This is the basis for the depth move "set an unreasonable target and present it as reachable".
- **Believing it's possible.** In 1939 George Dantzig arrived late to a statistics lecture, took two famous unsolved problems on the board for homework, and solved them.
- **Seeing someone else do it.** Others followed soon after Roger Bannister's four-minute mile in 1954. The article makes the same point about a recent sub-two-hour marathon: the runner-up also broke two hours in the same race.
- **Permission to fail.** *The Inner Game of Tennis* argues that trying too hard hurts performance. Linux was first announced as "just a hobby".
- **Experts underrate themselves outside their lane.** Michelangelo resisted painting the Sistine Chapel because he thought of himself as a sculptor.
- **Audacious deadlines.** Patrick Collison's "Fast" page lists major projects finished on timelines that sound impossible.

## How to use it

1. Name the specific belief that's lowering the goal ("models can't do X", "I'm not a Y person").
2. Check whether it's a **capability** limit (test it cheaply: run the smallest version of the rung) or a **belief** limit (no one has tried).
3. If it's a belief limit, keep the rung, frame it as reachable, and attach an outside judge so failure is cheap and visible.
4. If it's a capability limit you've confirmed by trying, drop one rung and record what failed. The next model may clear it.
