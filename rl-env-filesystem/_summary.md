A data layer for RL rollout sandboxes turns out to be a market-shaped design problem as much as a systems one: the buyers are RL environment companies with heterogeneous runtimes, so the winning point in the design space must meet them at a mount, not replace their fleet. The exploration enumerates seven axes (interface, capture granularity, addressing, materialization, cache topology, deployment, sharing semantics) across 14,400 nominal combinations, prunes with hard constraints derived from seven jobs, and lands on a checkpoint-native rollout store: lazy delivery of unmodified container images from a content-addressed multi-tier cache, copy-on-write diff capture at rollout stage boundaries, branchable snapshot trees, and the customer's own object storage bucket as the durable source of truth. The economics hinge on immutability, with the empirical foundation going back to [Slacker (USENIX FAST '16)](https://www.usenix.org/conference/fast16/technical-sessions/presentation/harter), which showed containers read only a small fraction of their image bytes. A cost model makes the flagship workflow concrete: re-grading 10,000 stored rollout checkpoints costs about $19 of compute against roughly $456 plus all the agent-loop model tokens to re-run them.

- Nearly every rollout artifact is immutable or append-only, which makes caching and dedup trivially correct and removes distributed write consistency from the rollout path entirely.
- Memory-state capture and VM forking are the strongest technology in the space but survive only as per-runtime adapters; requiring them turns every prospective customer into a competitor.
- Shared read-write filesystems across sandboxes actively conflict with re-gradability and audit, and belong to a different product.
