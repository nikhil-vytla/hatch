The interesting part is
where they touch.

Play from the start and watch the purple grains reach the pool. In the left branch they keep falling. In the right branch, a grain touching water becomes wood. The solid cell leaves a visible trace of the contact and changes the path of the grains behind it.

The added rule is deliberately small. It checks the four neighboring cells, changes the current cell and finishes its turn. The rest of the simulation still handles falling and sideways movement.

engine.ts · TypeScript excerpt
if (
  cell === 7 &&
  scene.rule.contact !== "none" &&
  adjacent.some((n) =>
    cells[n] === contacts[scene.rule.contact])
) {
  set(i, ids[scene.rule.becomes]);
  continue;
}

Actual contact branch, with line breaks adjusted. Material 7 is the configurable slot. The omitted contacts and ids maps resolve names to material numbers; set resets age and marks the cell as moved. Only the powder changes. The water remains.

What does the model decide here?

Nothing in this figure. Both rules are set explicitly and run locally. The full sandbox can ask Jev to interpret an instruction into a typed rule. That separate interpretation task has frozen labels and a protocol, but this example supplies no model accuracy result.

A changed shape is a result. It is not a physics claim.

These are grid rules, not a fluid solver. The engine alternates its horizontal scan direction, processes one cell at a time and checks immediate neighbors. The two branches can consume random numbers differently after they diverge. An equal starting seed does not guarantee that every later random choice remains paired.

This makes a useful place to explore rules and preserve a scene. It does not show that a model understands materials, or that the picture behaves like real powder and water.
